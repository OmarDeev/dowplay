1 · Overview

What students learn

Grid Manager turns the job of a power-grid engineer into a puzzle. Students build solar farms, wind turbines, batteries, pumped hydro and gas plants on a town map, and a chart shows, hour by hour, whether supply meets demand. Its eight levels run from Year 9 to Year 13.

By the end, students can

  • explain why electricity supply must meet demand every hour, not just on average
  • use E = P × t to work in MW and MWh
  • explain how storage moves energy from when it's made to when it's needed, and why it needs enough power and enough energy
  • compare sources by reliability, cost and capacity factor
  • evaluate trade-offs between cost, reliability and CO₂ emissions
  • explain demand-side response and long-duration storage

You will need

  • One device per pair (any browser)
  • A projector for the starters
  • Worksheet 1 or 2 for each student

Timing

Each lesson plan fills 60 minutes. The levels also work as a homework or a club activity: each takes 5–15 minutes.

2 · Teacher background

How the game works

Every hour, the game does this:

  1. Solar farms and wind turbines make what the weather allows.
  2. If that's more than the town needs, the spare charges the batteries (then pumped hydro). Anything left over is wasted.
  3. If it's less, the batteries help as much as they can (then pumped hydro), gas plants fill the rest, and anything still missing is a blackout.

The level's days repeat, so batteries start each day with whatever they had left at the end of it. That stops a design from "borrowing" energy it never made.

2 · Teacher background (continued)

Stars

1 star for keeping the lights on (and under the CO₂ limit), 2 for staying within the budget, 3 for matching the cheapest design found by our level checker. Students sometimes match it with a different mix; very occasionally they beat it.

What the model leaves out

Real grids also trade power with neighbouring regions and countries, use forecasts that can be wrong, and are planned in minutes rather than whole hours. In the game, storage is always used before gas (a simple rule real grid controllers improve on), and every day of a level repeats. These make good discussion points with older students.

Level by level

"3 stars" is the cheapest design our checker found. Keep it to yourself until students have had a go: the fun is in the search.

1

Sunny School

Year 9 · Supply must meet demand every hour
Watch for
Building for midday. Two farms look plenty at noon but fail at 8 am, when the sun is low and each farm makes only 2.1 MW.
3 stars, £90
3 solar farms on fields (two fields and one valley also works).
Ask
"Why does 8 am, not midday, decide how many farms you need?"
2

After Dark

Year 9 · Storage moves energy from day to night
Watch for
Plenty of solar but too few batteries: energy wasted at midday, blackout at night (Worksheet 1, Part C).
3 stars, £300
4 solar farms and 6 batteries (5 and 5 costs the same).
Ask
"Your batteries are full by lunchtime. What does that tell you?"
3

Windy Hill

Years 9–10 · Mixing sources; where you build
Watch for
Turbines in fields (half the wind of a hilltop), or ignoring wind because it costs more.
3 stars, £230
2 wind turbines on hilltops, 3 solar farms, 2 batteries.
Ask
"The same town cost £300 in After Dark. Why is it cheaper with wind?"
4

Evening Rush

Year 10 · Battery power (MW) vs energy (MWh)
Watch for
Enough stored energy but too few batteries to give 14 MW at 6 pm. The game says the batteries "still have energy" during the blackout.
3 stars, £370
7 batteries, with 4 solar farms and 1 wind turbine (or 4 wind turbines).
Ask
"Which limit did your design hit first, power or energy? How can you tell from the chart?"
5

Grey Skies

Years 10–11 · Designing for the worst day
Watch for
A grid that works on sunny Monday but not grey Tuesday, when each solar farm makes about a quarter as much.
3 stars, £390
3 wind turbines on hilltops, 3 solar farms, 6 batteries (3–6 solar farms with 6–3 batteries all cost the same).
Ask
"Which machines earn their keep on Tuesday?"
6

Backup Plan

Years 11–12 · Gas backup and CO₂ limits
Watch for
Gas running all evening (over the 4 t limit), or no gas at all and more batteries than needed.
3 stars, £409
6 wind turbines, 5 batteries and 1 gas plant that burns 4.7 MWh (1.9 t CO₂). With no CO₂ limit the cheapest grid costs £317 but releases 33.5 t; with no gas at all it costs £420.
Ask
"Is a 29% higher bill worth a 94% cut in CO₂? Who should pay?"
7

Smart Timing

Year 12 · Demand-side response
Watch for
Leaving the jobs in the evening, or moving them into the night when only a light wind blows.
3 stars, £280
Move the pumps, buses and oven into the morning and middle of the day; 4 solar farms, 1 wind turbine, 4 batteries. Without moving the jobs, the cheapest grid costs £390.
Ask
"What would make a real factory move its oven to midday?"
8

Still Week

Years 12–13 · Long-duration storage
Watch for
Batteries only (very expensive), turbines on land when the sea is windier, and forgetting pumped hydro gives out only 4 MW.
3 stars, £366
5 wind turbines at sea, 2 pumped hydro, 1 battery, 1 gas plant (3.3 t CO₂ over three days). Without pumped hydro the cheapest grid costs £696.
Ask
"What if Tuesday's calm lasted two days? What would you change?"

Lesson 1: Keeping the lights on

Objectives

  • Explain why supply must meet demand every hour.
  • Calculate energy using E = P × t, in MWh.
  • Explain how batteries store energy for later, and why they need enough power and enough energy.

Resources

  • Worksheet 1 (one each)
  • Devices, one per pair
  • Grid Manager on the projector
TimeActivity
Starter
10 min		Open Level 1, Sunny School on the board. "The school needs 5 MW at 8 am. A solar farm makes up to 4 MW. How many farms?" Most say two. Build two on fields and point at the chart: there's a blackout at 8 am, because in the early morning each farm makes only 2.1 MW. Add a third. Ask: why does the worst hour decide the design?
Main 1
15 min		Worksheet 1, Part A: power and energy calculations. Model question A1 first (4 MW × 3 h = 12 MWh). Go through A3 and A4 together: A4 is the key idea, a battery's power limit.
Main 2
25 min		Pairs play levels 1–4 and fill in the record table (Part B). Before each Run, one partner predicts what will happen. Circulate and ask: "Is your problem not enough energy, or not enough power?"
Plenary
10 min		Part C together: read the After Dark chart. Big question: "This design wastes 57 MWh and is still 17 MWh short. More solar, or more batteries?" Exit ticket: explain the difference between MW and MWh in one sentence.

Support

Start with levels 1–2 and use the in-game hints. Give the battery count for After Dark (6) and ask students to work out the solar.

Stretch

Calculate the batteries needed before building. Try Level 5, Grey Skies. Go for 3 stars on Windy Hill (£230).

Misconceptions to listen for

  • "Power and energy are the same thing." The battery power limit in Evening Rush separates them.
  • "More solar always helps." Not when the batteries are already full; then it's just wasted.
  • "Batteries make electricity." They only move energy in time, and lose 10% doing it.

Homework: Worksheet 1, Part D, and get 3 stars on Level 3.

Lesson 2: Designing a low-carbon grid

Objectives

  • Calculate capacity factor, efficiency and cost per MWh.
  • Evaluate trade-offs between cost, reliability and CO₂.
  • Explain demand-side response and long-duration storage.

Resources

  • Worksheet 2 (one each)
  • Devices, one per pair or small group
  • Grid Manager on the projector
TimeActivity
Starter
10 min		Show Level 6, Backup Plan. Reveal two designs: the cheapest grid with no CO₂ limit costs £317 a day and releases 33.5 t of CO₂; the cheapest that meets the 4 t limit costs £409 and releases 1.9 t. Ask: "Is 29% more cost worth 94% less CO₂? Who pays, and who benefits?"
Main 1
15 min		Worksheet 2, questions 1–4: capacity factor, cost per MWh, CO₂ and storage efficiency. Pairs check each other's working.
Main 2
25 min		Groups take one of levels 6, 7 or 8. Each finds its cheapest design, then reports to the class: its cost, its CO₂, and one thing that surprised them. Use questions 5–6 to guide Smart Timing and Still Week groups.
Plenary
10 min		Plan question 7 together: list evidence for and against batteries, pumped hydro, demand-side response and gas backup. Finish it as homework.

Support

Work through Q1 together. Give groups Smart Timing (Level 7) first: moving the jobs is visual and quick to understand.

Stretch

Compare the game's assumptions with a real grid: interconnectors, forecasts, minute-by-minute balancing. Look up the current carbon intensity of your country's grid and discuss what drives it.

Discussion prompts

  • Why might a country keep some gas plants even if it aims for net zero?
  • Smart Timing's flexible jobs are about a fifth of the town's demand. What in a real home could be flexible?
  • Pumped hydro needs hills and lakes. What other long-duration storage could a flat country use?

Homework: Worksheet 2, question 7 (6 marks).

Worksheet 1 answers

Part A

A14 MW × 3 h = 12 MWh.

A2a) 120 ÷ 40.8 = 2.94, so 3 solar farms.
b) Solar farms make nothing when the sun is down (1); the energy has to be stored when it's made, to use at night (1).

A3a) 40 ÷ 8 = 5 batteries.
b) 40 ÷ 0.9 = 44.4 MWh (1 for dividing by 0.9, 1 for the answer).

A4a) 14 − 0.15 = 13.85 MW; 13.85 ÷ 2 = 6.9, so 7 batteries (1 for working, 1 for rounding up).
b) 7 × 8 = 56 MWh.
c) Each battery can only give out 2 MW, however full it is (1). The limit at 6 pm is power, how fast energy can be delivered, not how much is stored (1).

Part C

C106:00 (6 am), when the sun starts to make more than the town needs.

C2By 13:00 (they finish filling between 12:00 and 13:00).

C3Three batteries can only take in 6 MW together, 2 MW each (1); the extra solar power has nowhere to go, so it's wasted (1).

C4Starts at 23:00, when the batteries run empty (1); ends at 06:00 when the sun comes up, about 7 hours later (1).

C5More batteries (1). The batteries are already full by 13:00, so extra solar energy would be wasted too (1). More batteries can store the spare midday energy for the night (1). The cheapest working design uses 4 solar farms and 6 batteries.

Part D

D1Any four: wind blows at night, when solar makes nothing; energy used as it's made doesn't need storing; storage is expensive (£30 a day per 8 MWh) and loses 10%; so the design needs far fewer batteries (2 instead of 6); hilltops give turbines twice the output of fields.

Worksheet 2 answers

1a) 30 ÷ (3 × 24) = 30 ÷ 72 = 41.7%.
b) 15 ÷ 72 = 20.8%. The same machine makes half as much energy on a poor site, so the site decides whether it pays for itself.
c) Monday 33.9 ÷ 96 = 35.3%; Tuesday 8.9 ÷ 96 = 9.3%.

2a) Monday £30 ÷ 33.9 = £0.88 per MWh; Tuesday £30 ÷ 8.9 = £3.37 per MWh.
b) £30 ÷ 8 = £3.75 per MWh (more once the 10% loss is counted).
c) Wind keeps working on grey Tuesday and at night (1); solar on a grey day costs nearly as much per MWh as storing energy (1); every MWh of wind used directly avoids storage costs and losses (1).

3a) 4 ÷ 0.4 = 10 MWh.
b) 83.7 × 0.4 = 33.5 t.
c) Cost (409 − 317) ÷ 317 = +29%; CO₂ (33.5 − 1.9) ÷ 33.5 = −94%.
d) £92 ÷ 31.6 t = about £2.90 per tonne avoided (in game money).

4a) 45 ÷ 0.75 = 60 MWh.
b) 45 ÷ 0.9 = 50 MWh.
c) Pumped hydro £60 ÷ 60 MWh = £1.00 per MWh; batteries £30 ÷ 8 MWh = £3.75 per MWh.
d) Two pumped hydro plants give out only 8 MW (1), so at the 12 MW evening peak the battery and gas plant supply the extra power (1).

5a) 4 × 3 = 12 MWh.
b) 12 ÷ 0.9 = 13.3 MWh in (2); 12 ÷ 8 = 1.5, so 2 batteries (1).
c) Any two: cheaper electricity at midday (time-of-use tariffs); smart chargers and timers; paying businesses to shift demand; automatic control of heat pumps or water heaters.

6a) 171 − 34.7 = 136.3 MWh.
b) 136.3 − 10 = 126.3 MWh (in the game's design, 128 MWh came from storage and 8.4 MWh from gas).
c) Still, grey spells last days (1); batteries are sized for hours and are expensive per MWh, while pumped hydro stores days of energy cheaply (1).

Indicative content

Batteries
  • Efficient (keep 90%) and quick to respond
  • Good for hours and peaks (Evening Rush)
  • Expensive per MWh stored (£3.75), so poor for days
Pumped hydro
  • Cheap per MWh stored (£1); covers days (Still Week)
  • Loses 25%; limited power (4 MW each)
  • Needs suitable hills and lakes
Demand-side response
  • Cheapest: no machines and no losses (Smart Timing: £390 → £280)
  • Only works for flexible demand (about a fifth there)
  • Needs people and businesses to change habits or tariffs
Gas backup
  • Dispatchable: works whatever the weather; cheap to build
  • Fuel costs and 0.4 t CO₂ per MWh (Backup Plan: 33.5 t without a limit)
  • A little gas for the worst hours can be the cheapest way to stay reliable

Credit references to real-world factors: interconnectors to other countries, forecasting, other long-duration storage (hydrogen, compressed air), and who pays the extra cost.
