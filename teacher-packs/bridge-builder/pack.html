1 · Overview

What students learn

Bridge Builder turns structural engineering into a puzzle. Students build a bridge from road, wood, steel and cables, then drive a car, van, truck, lorry or crane across it. The game works out the force in every beam the way structural engineers do (the direct stiffness method): beams snap when they're pulled too hard, crush or buckle when they're squashed, and bridges without triangles fold up. Its eight levels run from Year 8 to Year 13.

By the end, students can

  • explain why triangles make structures rigid
  • identify tension and compression in a truss
  • choose materials by strength, weight and cost
  • explain buckling: long beams are weak when squashed
  • explain why deeper trusses carry bigger loads
  • resolve forces in a frame and in a cable-stayed bridge

You will need

  • One device per pair (any browser)
  • A projector for the starters
  • Worksheet 1 or 2 for each student

Timing

Each lesson plan fills 60 minutes. The levels also work as a homework or a club activity: each takes 5–15 minutes.

How the game works

The game analyses the bridge like a structural engineer:

  1. Beams join points on a grid, and one square is 4 m. Road must cross the gap along the dashed line.
  2. As the vehicle drives across, the game works out the force in every beam, including the bridge's own weight.
  3. A beam breaks if it's pulled harder than its strength, or squashed harder than it can take before crushing or buckling. A bridge without triangles folds up.

The materials

MaterialPull (kN)Squash (kN)Weight (kN per square)Cost
Road2002004£15
Wood7060 (30 diagonal)1.5£8 (£11 diagonal)
Steel200180 (90 diagonal)3£16 (£23 diagonal)
Cable2000: goes slack0.5£5 per square of length

Squashed beams buckle at a force that falls with the square of their length: wood buckles at 60 ÷ L² kN and steel at 180 ÷ L² kN, with L in squares. A diagonal is √2 squares long, so it buckles at half the force of a straight beam. Vehicles: car 20 kN, van 30 kN, truck 60 kN, lorry 80 kN, crane 100 kN (10 kN is about 1 tonne).

Reading the screen

Tick Show forces to colour every beam: blue is pulled (tension), orange is squashed (compression) and red is over 100%, so it would break. Percentages on the hardest-working beams show the worst moment as the vehicle crosses. When it drives, the bending is drawn 40 times bigger so students can see it, and a bridge that fails collapses.

Stars

1 star for getting across, 2 for staying within the budget, 3 for matching the cheapest design our checker found.

What the model leaves out

Joints are pins, so beams carry only pulling or squashing forces (real joints are bolted or welded and carry some bending). The vehicle crosses slowly, so there's no bouncing, wind or vibration, and materials never fatigue. Good discussion points with older students.

Key words

Tension: pulled. Compression: squashed. Truss: a frame of triangles. Buckling: a squashed beam bowing sideways and failing. Span: the gap a bridge crosses. Chord: the top or bottom beams of a truss. Pier: a support in the middle. Bending moment: the turning effect of the load, which the chords resist.

When a bridge fails

The game says why, which makes a good discussion starter:

  • Wobbly! Part of the bridge can fold like a hinge, so it needs more triangles.
  • Snap! A beam was pulled harder than it can take (tension).
  • Crunch! A beam was crushed (compression).
  • Buckled! A squashed beam bent sideways. Longer beams buckle at lower forces.

After a failed run, red rings mark the beams that broke or the joints that wobbled.

Real bridges to show the class

  • Levels 2–3: truss footbridges and railway bridges. Count the triangles.
  • Level 4: the Iron Bridge in Shropshire (1779), an arch under the road.
  • Level 5: Pratt trusses, used on many railway bridges.
  • Levels 6 and 8: the Forth Bridge (1890), deepest over its piers, where the forces are biggest.
  • Level 7: the Queensferry Crossing (2017), a cable-stayed bridge across the same estuary.

Lesson 1: Triangles, tension and compression

Objectives

  • Explain why triangles make structures rigid.
  • Identify beams in tension and compression.
  • Choose stronger materials where forces are biggest.

Resources

  • Worksheet 1 (one each)
  • Devices, one per pair
  • Bridge Builder on the projector
  • Optional: straws or card strips and split pins to build a square and a triangle
TimeActivity
Starter
10 min		Open Level 1, Mind the Gap. Lay two pieces of road and drive the car: the bridge folds. Ask why, then add one diagonal from an anchor bolt on the cliff to the middle joint and drive again. If you have straws or card strips, let students push a square and a triangle out of shape.
Main 1
15 min		Worksheet 1, Part A: which frames are rigid, pulled or squashed, vehicle weights and bridge costs.
Main 2
25 min		Pairs play levels 1–4 with Show forces ticked and fill in the record table (Part B). Before driving, one partner predicts which beam will work hardest.
Plenary
10 min		Part C together: the Push and Pull forces picture. Why steel on the diagonals and wood elsewhere? Exit ticket: name a structure near school that uses triangles, and say why.

Support

Use the in-game hints. Ask students to find a square in their bridge and add one diagonal to it.

Stretch

Find the cheapest working design for Push and Pull (3 stars at £192). Try Level 5, Buckle Up.

Misconceptions to listen for

  • "More beams always make a stronger bridge." Every beam adds weight and cost; the right beams in the right places matter.
  • "Every beam is squashed by the load." Some are pulled: in a truss, the top is squashed and the bottom is stretched.
  • "Steel is always better." Steel costs twice as much and weighs twice as much as wood.

Homework: Worksheet 1, Part D, and get 3 stars on Level 2.

Lesson 2: Forces in structures

Objectives

  • Resolve forces in a simple frame and in a cable.
  • Explain buckling, and why long beams should be in tension.
  • Explain why deeper trusses and middle supports reduce forces.

Resources

  • Worksheet 2 (one each)
  • Devices, one per pair
  • Bridge Builder on the projector
TimeActivity
Starter
10 min		Show Level 5, Buckle Up with Show forces ticked. Build a truss with the long diagonals squashed and drive: they buckle. Ask why a beam that's 41% longer is only half as strong when squashed. Then flip the diagonals so they're pulled.
Main 1
15 min		Worksheet 2, questions 1–3: resolving forces in a frame, buckling, and chord forces in a deep truss.
Main 2
25 min		Pairs play levels 5–8. Use question 4 to guide Cable-Stayed and question 5 to discuss material choices.
Plenary
10 min		Plan question 6 together: three ways to carry a crane across a river. Finish it as homework.

Support

Draw force triangles for question 1 together. Use the colours in the game: blue pulled, orange squashed.

Stretch

Go for 3 stars on Go Deeper (£686) or Heavy Haul (£353). Research the Forth Bridge (a cantilever truss) or the Millau Viaduct (cable-stayed).

Misconceptions to listen for

  • "A cable at a gentler angle is under less strain." The flatter the cable, the more tension it needs to hold the same weight.
  • "Buckling is the same as crushing." Buckling is a sideways bowing that happens well before the material itself crushes.
  • "A longer bridge just needs proportionally more beams." The bending effect grows faster than the span, because a longer bridge is also heavier.

Homework: Worksheet 2, question 6 (6 marks).

Forces in structures

Name:Class:Date:
Force F at angle θ to the horizontal: horizontal part F cos θ, vertical part F sin θ.   sin 45° = cos 45° ≈ 0.707
Buckling: a squashed beam's strength is proportional to 1 ÷ length².
Load W at the middle of a span L: biggest bending moment M = W L ÷ 4.   Force in each chord ≈ M ÷ depth.
Wood: pulls 70 kN, squashes 60 kN (30 kN diagonal).   Steel: pulls 200 kN, squashes 180 kN (90 kN diagonal).

1Resolving forces. Two beams at 45° meet at the top and are tied together at the bottom. A load of 60 kN pushes down on the top joint. Ignore the beams' own weight.

a) Each sloping beam carries half the load vertically. Show that the force in each sloping beam is about 42 kN. Is it pulled or squashed? [3]

b) What is the force in the tie at the bottom? Is it pulled or squashed? [2]

c) The sloping beams are diagonals in Bridge Builder. Should they be wood or steel? Should the tie be wood or steel? [2]

b) Calculate the force in the top chord if the truss is 4 m deep, and if it is 8 m deep. [2]

Worksheet 2 answers

1a) Each beam supports 30 kN vertically (1): F sin 45° = 30, so F = 30 ÷ 0.707 (1) = 42.4 kN, squashed (1).
b) Horizontal part of each beam's force: 42.4 × cos 45° = 30 kN (1); the tie stops the feet spreading, so it's pulled (1).
c) The sloping beams need steel: 42 kN is more than a wooden diagonal's 30 kN but less than steel's 90 kN (1). The tie can be wood: 30 kN is less than wood's 70 kN pulling strength (1).

2a) Strength is proportional to 1 ÷ L²: 60 ÷ (√2)² (1) = 60 ÷ 2 = 30 kN (1).
b) 60 ÷ 2² = 15 kN.
c) Long beams are weak when squashed but not when pulled, so the long diagonals are put in tension (1) and the short uprights, which resist buckling better, take the compression (1).

3a) M = 80 × 32 ÷ 4 = 640 kN m.
b) 640 ÷ 4 = 160 kN; 640 ÷ 8 = 80 kN.
c) Wood: no at either depth (more than 60 kN) (1). Steel: yes at both (less than 180 kN) (1).
d) Chord force = moment ÷ depth, so a deeper truss has smaller forces (1), needing lighter, cheaper beams where the moment is biggest (1).

4a) T = 20 ÷ sin θ: 45° → 28.3 kN; 30° → 40 kN; 15° → 77.3 kN.
b) Flatter cables need much more tension for the same weight (1); tall towers make the cables steeper, so they need less tension and can be thinner and cheaper (1).

5a) Wood 70 ÷ 8 = 8.75 kN per £; steel 200 ÷ 16 = 12.5 kN per £ (1): steel (1).
b) Many beams carry small forces, and a wooden beam that's strong enough costs half as much (1); wood is half the weight (1.5 kN against 3 kN per square), so it adds less load to the bridge (1).

Indicative content

Deep truss
  • The bending effect grows with the span, so a 40 m span needs a deep, heavy truss
  • Depth cuts chord forces (Go Deeper: 8 m deep, £686 for 32 m)
  • Lots of steel: expensive and heavy
Cable-stayed
  • Cables are light and strong, but only pull (Cable-Stayed: £374)
  • Needs tall towers so the cables are steep
  • The road between the cables is squashed
Support in the middle
  • Halving the span cuts the bending effect enormously
  • Lighter, cheaper beams (Heavy Haul: £353 using the rock)
  • Needs foundations in the river; can block boats

Practical follow-up: the paper bridge challenge

A one-lesson D&T or science practical that tests the same ideas with real materials.

  1. Groups get paper straws (or rolled-up newspaper), masking tape and a 30 cm gap between two desks.
  2. Set prices, for example £10 a straw and £2 for each 10 cm of tape, and a budget of £200.
  3. Hang a paper cup from the middle of the bridge and add coins or washers until it fails. Record the load and the cost.
  4. Score each bridge as load ÷ cost. Which designs used triangles? Did they fail by snapping, crushing or buckling?
  5. Photograph the failure and label which straws were pulled and which were squashed.
