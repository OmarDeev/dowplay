1 · Overview

What students learn

Gear Garage turns mechanisms into puzzles. Students place gears on a pegboard to connect a motor to a machine (a wheel, a conveyor, a fan, a winch, a camshaft, a turntable, a garage door, a lift) so that it turns at the right speed, in the right direction and with enough force, within a budget. The gears mesh, jam and stall exactly as real ones would. Its eight levels run from Year 8 to Year 13.

By the end, students can

  • explain why meshing gears turn in opposite directions, and use idlers
  • calculate output speeds from the numbers of teeth
  • explain the trade between speed and torque
  • show that idlers don't change the overall ratio
  • calculate the ratio of a compound gear train
  • use v = ω r for a rack and pinion, and P = T ω with efficiency

You will need

  • One device per pair (any browser)
  • A projector for the starters
  • Worksheet 1 or 2 for each student

Timing

Each lesson plan fills 60 minutes. The levels also work as a homework or a club activity: each takes 5–15 minutes.

2 · Teacher background

How the game works

The pegboard follows real gear geometry:

  1. An N-tooth gear has a radius of N ÷ 8 holes, so two gears mesh when their axles are (N₁ + N₂) ÷ 8 holes apart: two 16-tooth gears mesh 4 holes apart.
  2. Meshing gears turn in opposite directions. Their teeth move at the same speed, so speed × teeth is the same for both gears.
  3. Three gears meshing in a ring jam. The motor stalls if the machine needs more torque than it can give.

A machine's speed counts as right within 1% either way. Torque (turning force) goes up by the same factor the speed goes down.

The parts

PartDetails
Gears8, 16, 24, 32 and 40 teeth (radius 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 holes), costing £2, £3, £4, £5 and £6
MotorTurns at a set speed (rpm) and direction, and can give up to a set torque (N·m)
MachinesEach needs a speed, a direction or a torque (some need two of these)
Compound gearsLevels 6 and 8: two gears stacked on one axle turn together at the same speed
RackLevel 7: a straight bar of teeth. Holes are 1 cm apart there, so a gear's radius is N ÷ 8 cm
EfficiencyLevel 8: each mesh passes on only 95% of the power

Reading the screen

The gears really turn, with their teeth meshing. Ticking Show the numbers labels every gear with its speed (rpm) and direction, and every machine with what it needs, what it's getting and its torque. The worksheet figures are taken straight from that view.

Stars

1 star for a working machine, 2 for staying within the budget, 3 for matching the cheapest design we know. Students sometimes match it with a different set of gears.

What the model leaves out

Real gears lose a little energy at every mesh (the game only models this on level 8), have a little slack (backlash) between teeth, and need stronger, wider teeth for big torques. In the game, gears never wear or break.

Key words

Mesh: two gears' teeth interlocking. Driver / driven: the gear that pushes, and the gear being pushed. Idler: a gear in between that changes direction but not the overall speed. Torque: turning force (N·m). rpm: revolutions per minute. Compound train: two gears on one axle, so the ratios multiply. Rack and pinion: a gear driving a straight bar of teeth. Efficiency: power out ÷ power in.

Level by level

"3 stars" is the cheapest design our checker found. Keep it to yourself until students have had a go: the fun is in the search.

1

First Gear

Year 8 · Meshing gears turn each other
Watch for
Gears that don't quite touch, or that overlap and won't fit: they must be exactly the right distance apart.
3 stars, £6
Two gears whose teeth add up to 32 (16 + 16, or 8 + 24), since the axles are 4 holes apart. Three 8-tooth gears also cost £6.
Ask
"Which way does the wheel turn compared with the motor? Why?"
2

Same Way Round

Year 8 · Idler gears change direction
Watch for
Two gears (the drum turns the wrong way), or an even number of gears in the chain.
3 stars, £8
An 8-tooth gear on the motor, a 24-tooth idler, and an 8-tooth gear on the drum.
Ask
"What would a fourth gear do?"
3

Need for Speed

Years 8–9 · Speeding up with gears
Watch for
A big gear on the fan, which slows it down.
3 stars, £6
A 24-tooth gear on the motor and an 8-tooth gear on the fan: 20 rpm × 24 ÷ 8 = 60 rpm.
Ask
"What does the fan give up to go faster?" (Torque: 1.67 N·m instead of the motor's 5.)
4

Heavy Lifting

Year 9 · Trading speed for torque
Watch for
Gears that keep the speed the same: the 2 N·m motor stalls against the 5 N·m winch.
3 stars, £7
An 8-tooth gear on the motor and a 32-tooth gear on the winch: 4 times slower (7.5 rpm) and 4 times the torque (8 N·m).
Ask
"What's the smallest slow-down that would work?" (2.5 times.)
5

Round the Engine

Years 9–10 · Idlers don't change the ratio
Watch for
The camshaft turning the wrong way, or a chain that crashes into the engine block.
3 stars, £15
An 8-tooth gear on the crankshaft, then 24, 16 and 16, and a 16-tooth gear on the camshaft: 40 rpm becomes 20 rpm, clockwise.
Ask
"Why don't the gears in the middle change the camshaft's speed?"
6

Compound Gears

Years 10–11 · Ratios multiply
Watch for
Trying one pair of gears: the biggest single slow-down is 40 ÷ 8 = 5 times.
3 stars, £12
8 teeth driving 24, then an 8-tooth gear stacked on the 24's axle driving a 24-tooth gear on the turntable: 36 rpm ÷ 9 = 4 rpm, with 45 N·m of torque.
Ask
"How would you slow it down 15 times?" (8 → 24, then 8 → 40.)
7

Garage Door

Years 11–12 · Rack and pinion, v = ω r
Watch for
Thinking the gear against the rack decides the speed. In a simple chain, it's the gear on the motor.
3 stars, £8
A 24-tooth gear (radius 3 cm) on the motor and a second 24-tooth gear against the rack: π rad/s × 3 cm = 9.4 cm/s.
Ask
"Why doesn't the size of the gear on the rack matter?"
8

Every Mesh Counts

Years 12–13 · Efficiency multiplies
Watch for
A simple train with idlers: every mesh loses 5%, and the motor stalls.
3 stars, £10
A compound train, 8 → 16 then 8 → 16: four times slower in just two meshes, giving 15 rpm and 3.61 N·m.
Ask
"What would one more mesh do?" (3.43 N·m: the motor stalls.)
A note on "gear ratio". Textbooks usually define the gear ratio as driven teeth ÷ driver teeth (how many times slower the output turns). The game's hints talk about how many times faster the output turns (driver ÷ driven). This pack avoids the ambiguity by writing output speed = input speed × driver teeth ÷ driven teeth. Use whichever convention your course uses.

Lesson 1: Gears, speed and direction

Objectives

  • Predict which way each gear in a chain turns.
  • Work out a gear's speed from the numbers of teeth.
  • Explain how gears trade speed for turning force.

Resources

  • Worksheet 1 (one each)
  • Devices, one per pair
  • Gear Garage on the projector
TimeActivity
Starter
10 min		Open Level 1, First Gear. Ask which way the wheel will turn if one gear on the motor meshes with one on the wheel. Build it and watch: opposite ways. Then Level 2, Same Way Round: "How do we make the drum turn the same way as the motor?" Take ideas before adding an idler.
Main 1
15 min		Worksheet 1, Part A: directions in a chain, speeding up and slowing down, and how far apart gears must be.
Main 2
25 min		Pairs play levels 1–4 and fill in the record table (Part B). Before pressing Check, one partner predicts the output's speed and direction.
Plenary
10 min		Part C together: the Round the Engine figure. Why do the three 16-tooth gears all turn at 20 rpm? Exit ticket: what do you gain and lose when a small gear drives a big one?

Support

Use the in-game hints. Ask students to count along the chain out loud: "clockwise, anticlockwise, clockwise…"

Stretch

Tick Show the numbers and predict every gear's speed before checking. Try Level 5, Round the Engine.

Misconceptions to listen for

  • "A bigger gear is always faster." A big gear driving a small one speeds it up; a small gear driving a big one slows it down.
  • "Idler gears change the speed." Only the first and last gears decide the overall speed in a simple chain.
  • "Faster means stronger." Gears trade one for the other: slower output, more torque.

Homework: Worksheet 1, Part D, and get 3 stars on Level 4.

Lesson 2: Gear trains, torque and power

Objectives

  • Calculate the ratios of simple and compound gear trains.
  • Use v = ω r for a rack and pinion.
  • Use P = T ω and efficiency to explain why fewer meshes are better.

Resources

  • Worksheet 2 (one each)
  • Devices, one per pair
  • Gear Garage on the projector
TimeActivity
Starter
10 min		Show Level 6, Compound Gears: "The turntable must turn 9 times slower, but the biggest pair only gives 5 times. What can we do?" Show a gear stacked on another's axle, and how the two slow-downs multiply.
Main 1
15 min		Worksheet 2, questions 1–2: idlers and compound trains, using the game's numbers.
Main 2
25 min		Pairs play levels 5–8. Use questions 3–4 to guide Garage Door and Every Mesh Counts: calculate first, then build.
Plenary
10 min		Plan question 5 together: designing a gearbox. Finish it as homework.

Support

Give students a table to fill in: gear, teeth, speed, direction. In Garage Door, convert rpm to rad/s together first.

Stretch

Find a design for Every Mesh Counts that isn't compound, and explain why it stalls. Research how a bicycle's gears or a clock's gear train work.

Misconceptions to listen for

  • "Gears create energy." They trade speed for torque; power out is never more than power in.
  • "The gear on the rack sets the door speed." In a simple chain every gear's teeth move at the same speed.
  • "rpm can go straight into v = ω r." ω must be in radians per second: rpm × 2π ÷ 60.

Homework: Worksheet 2, question 5 (6 marks).

a) Why does the stacked 8-tooth gear turn at 12 rpm? [1]

b) A 24-tooth gear on the motor has a radius of 3 cm. How fast do its teeth move? [1]

c) Which of the 8-, 16-, 24- and 32-tooth gears on the motor gives a door speed of 9–10 cm/s? Show your working. [2]

Worksheet 1 answers

Part A

A1a) B anticlockwise, C clockwise, D anticlockwise (2 marks for all three).
b) Add one more gear (an idler) anywhere in the chain, or remove one, so there's an odd number of gears.

A2a) 24 ÷ 8 = 3 times.
b) 20 × 3 = 60 rpm.

A3a) 30 × 8 ÷ 32 = 7.5 rpm.
b) Four times slower, so four times the torque (1): 2 × 4 = 8 N·m (1).

A4a) 32 ÷ 8 = 4 holes. b) 32 ÷ 8 = 4 holes. c) 40 ÷ 8 = 5 holes.

Part C

C140 × 8 ÷ 24 (1) = 13.3 rpm (1).

C2Gears of the same size meshing together turn at the same speed.

C3Only the first and last gears decide the speed (1): 40 × 8 ÷ 16 = 20 rpm, half the speed (1).

C4Yes (1): each mesh reverses the direction, and with five gears (an odd number) the last turns the same way as the first (1).

Part D

D1In a low gear the pedals turn more times for each turn of the wheel (1), so the wheel turns slower but with more torque (1), making it easier to push up the hill; the rider gives up speed (1).

Worksheet 2 answers

1a) 40 × 8 ÷ 16 = 20 rpm.
b) Going through the 24: 40 × 8 ÷ 24 = 13.3 rpm, then 13.3 × 24 ÷ 16 = 20 rpm (1). The 24s cancel: 40 × (8 ÷ 24) × (24 ÷ 16) = 40 × 8 ÷ 16 (1).

2a) It's on the same axle as the 24-tooth gear, so they turn together.
b) 24 ÷ 8 = 3 at each stage (1); 3 × 3 = 9 times: 36 → 12 → 4 rpm (1).
c) 5 × 9 = 45 N·m (as the game shows).
d) 8 → 24 (3 times) (1), then a stacked 8 → 40 (5 times): 3 × 5 = 15 (1).

3a) 30 × 2π ÷ 60 = π ≈ 3.14 rad/s.
b) v = 3.14 × 3 = 9.4 cm/s.
c) Radii 1, 2, 3 and 4 cm give 3.1, 6.3, 9.4 and 12.6 cm/s (1); only the 24-tooth gear works (1).
d) Meshing teeth move at the same speed (1), so in a simple chain every gear's teeth, and the rack, move at the motor gear's tooth speed (1).

4a) ω = 60 × 2π ÷ 60 = 2π rad/s (1); P = 1 × 2π = 6.3 W (1).
b) 60 ÷ 15 = 4 times (1); 1 × 4 = 4 N·m (1).
c) 4 × 0.95 × 0.95 (1) = 3.61 N·m, just enough (1).
d) 4 × 0.95³ = 3.43 N·m (1), less than 3.6 N·m, so the motor stalls (1).

Indicative content

Single pair
  • A 30 times slow-down needs one gear 30 times bigger, e.g. 8 and 240 teeth
  • Very large and heavy
  • Only one mesh, so efficient; output turns the opposite way
Simple train with idlers
  • Idlers don't change the ratio, so it's no smaller (Round the Engine)
  • Each extra mesh wastes energy (Every Mesh Counts)
  • Useful only to bridge a gap or change direction
Compound train
  • Ratios multiply, e.g. 5 × 6 = 30 in two meshes (Compound Gears)
  • Small and efficient: 0.95² ≈ 90%
  • Two meshes, so the output turns the same way as the motor

Credit references to real examples: car gearboxes, clocks, bicycles, and to the extra torque the output gets (30 times, less losses).
